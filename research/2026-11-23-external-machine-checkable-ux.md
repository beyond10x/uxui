# External research: machine-checkable UI/UX quality

Date 2026-11-23. Public web only; generic search terms. "[Inference]" marks my own reading, not a source claim. "[Secondary]" marks a blog or aggregator I did not trace to the primary source. Search-snippet figures were not all re-read in the primary source.

## 1. Formal, computable usability models

- KLM (Card, Moran, Newell 1980) sums operator times: K keystroke, P point, H home, D draw, M mental preparation, R system response. Original paper: https://iihm.imag.fr/blanch/ens/2010-2011/M1/EIHM/cours/1980-Card-KLM.pdf (CMU copy: https://iiif.library.cmu.edu/file/Newell_box00072_fld05090_doc0005/Newell_box00072_fld05090_doc0005.pdf)
- Operator values quoted by the sources: M = 1.35 s (SD about 1.1 s); P = 1.1 s flat, not Fitts; K = 0.2 s for an average skilled typist (depends on typing speed); H = 0.4 s is quoted by secondary sources only. https://web.mit.edu/6.813/www/sp17/classes/09-more-efficiency/ and https://www.usabilitybok.org/klm-goms/
- Reported fit in the original paper: RMS error 21% on individual tasks (23% in a robustness check). A single-time-per-operator model got 34%. Error is roughly proportional to duration. Prediction covers an error-free expert path.
- GOMS and KLM are used with a 20% acceptable-error rule of thumb. https://www.usabilitybok.org/klm-goms/
- CogTool (CMU/IBM) builds a KLM from a storyboard plus a demonstrated task and runs it in ACT-R (ACT-Simple) to predict skilled-user time. It also includes the distance between elements. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1985793.1985890
- CogTool accuracy: usually quoted at about 10% of observed time (John et al. 2004; the paper itself did not surface in my search, [Secondary]). A KLM study on handheld devices reported under 8% error. https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~luluo/Courses/05811Report.pdf
- Counter-evidence: three tasks on three website alternatives. Mean error in the predicted difference between interfaces was 5.49 s for KLM, 3.98 s for GOMS, 3.49 s for CogTool. The authors conclude these models cannot reliably predict differences between alternatives. https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/predicting-human-performance-differences-on-multiple-interface-al/ A Groningen PACS study found GOMS correlated better than CogTool: https://www.rug.nl/staff/f.cnossen/goms-cogtool-baproj.pdf
- [Inference] KLM is good for absolute magnitude of a known path and weak for ranking two close designs. Use it as a lower-is-better count (an "actions per fix" ratchet), not as a prediction of seconds.
- Fitts's law: movement time = a + b * log2(D/W + 1) (Shannon form, MacKenzie 1992). ISO 9241-411 gives throughput in bits/s with an effective-width correction. Mouse throughput in ISO-conforming studies is 3.7 to 4.9 bits/s; non-conforming studies vary much more. https://www.yorku.ca/mack/hhci2018.html and https://www.yorku.ca/mack/ijhcs2004.html
- Fitts criticism: Drewes argues the Shannon form is unfounded and recommends Fitts's original formula. http://www.cip.ifi.lmu.de/~drewes/science/fitts/LectureFittsLaw_v2.pdf
- [Inference] A tool can compute D and W from DOM bounding boxes of the controls on a task path. The coefficients a and b are per device and must be calibrated, so absolute seconds are not portable. The pair (ID, count of pointing moves) is.
- Tools that compute from a UI: KLM Form Analyzer (parses a web form, applies KLM plus Fitts): https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249644073_KLM_Form_Analyzer_Automated_Evaluation_of_Web_Form_Filling_Tasks_Using_Human_Performance_Models . CogTool-Helper (builds CogTool models from GUI functional test tools): https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1209&context=computerscidiss
- I found no tool that derives KLM from Playwright or Selenium scripts. [Inference] That is a build opportunity: a recorded scripted bot trace already gives the operator sequence (clicks, keystrokes, scroll, wait).
- Agent-KLM (arXiv 2610.05047, 2026, terminal UIs): an LLM agent acts as the user. Cost is split into interpretation and operation. Task outcomes are checked by actor-independent oracles. 84 tasks, 15 TUIs, 6 humans. Humans and agents had similar success rates but failed on different tasks; operation-cost rankings correlated across configurations. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05047 (abstract only read)
- Reading load: Brysbaert 2019 meta-analysis, 190 studies, silent reading 238 wpm for English non-fiction, 260 for fiction, 183 aloud; scanning is a different mode. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X19300786 [Inference] A word-count/238 estimate gives a defensible reading-seconds metric.
- "Glance cost" has no standard formal model that I found. [Inference] Closest: ACT-R eye-movement and visual-search modules inside CogTool, or a count of fixation-worthy regions.

## 2. Machine-checkable accessibility beyond axe

- ACT Rules Format 1.1 (W3C): a rule has a unique id, an input aspect, applicability, expectations, and a mapping to requirements (WCAG SC, level). Outcomes are passed, failed, cantTell, inapplicable, untested. Rules are atomic or composite. https://www.w3.org/TR/act-rules-format/ (1.0 Recommendation, 2019: https://www.w3.org/TR/2019/REC-act-rules-format-1.0-20191031/)
- ACT implementation consistency: W3C lists axe-core 4.10.2 with 26 consistent rules. Deque publishes reports. https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/act/implementations/axe-core , https://github.com/dequelabs/act-reports-axe
- Independent coverage test of axe-core against the ACT examples: caught 145 of 387 failing examples and 22 of 36 criteria scored zero. [Secondary], the author's own stricter method. https://jangwook.net/en/blog/en/act-rules-axe-coverage-wcag-sc-2026/
- [Inference] axe's "incomplete / needs review" maps to ACT cantTell. A contract should treat cantTell as its own state, not as pass.
- Deque study (2021): over 2,000 audits, about 13,000 pages, nearly 300,000 issues. axe-core rules alone found 57.38% of issues by volume; with Intelligent Guided Tests 80.39%. https://www.deque.com/blog/automated-testing-study-identifies-57-percent-of-digital-accessibility-issues/
- The 57% counts issues, not success criteria. By criteria, secondary sources say 32% of WCAG 2.1 A+AA SC are testable by scan [Secondary], and Accessible.org claims 13% of WCAG 2.2 AA can be detected with high accuracy and 42% by no scanner [Secondary]. Deque's data are its own customers' first audits, so there is selection bias. https://digitalmatters.me/web-design/what-is-deque-axe/
- UK GDS tool audit (2017/2018): 142 to 143 deliberate barriers on one test page, 10 to 13 tools. The best single tool found 40%, the worst 13%, all combined 71%. It is one artificial page. https://alphagov.github.io/accessibility-tool-audit/ (blog: https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2017/02/24/what-we-found-when-we-tested-tools-on-the-worlds-least-accessible-webpage/)
- Playwright `toMatchAriaSnapshot` (v1.49+): YAML of the accessibility tree, compared with a template, partial matching allowed, order-sensitive, retries until match. `locator.ariaSnapshot()` returns the raw YAML. https://playwright.dev/docs/aria-snapshots
- [Inference] The aria snapshot is the most directly declarative a11y/structure contract on the list: a hand-written YAML spec of roles, names, states. It says nothing about visual layout.
- Guidepup (the tool is "Guidepup", not "Guidepost"): automates real NVDA (Windows), VoiceOver (macOS), Orca (Linux) via COM or AppleScript; API `start`, `next`, `perform`, `lastSpokenPhrase`, `spokenPhraseLog`. Runs serially and needs a real OS session. https://github.com/guidepup/guidepup , https://www.guidepup.dev/docs/screen-reader-testing/testing-with-real-screen-readers
- Guidepup caveat: desktop VoiceOver is not iPhone VoiceOver, NVDA is not TalkBack. https://github.com/esaldivar/parenthesis/issues/281
- [Inference] The spoken-phrase log is a deterministic text trace of a screen-reader user, assertable like any other log. Cost: heavy CI (Windows/macOS runners).

## 3. Declarative UI/UX contracts, task models, state machines

- ConcurTaskTrees (Paterno 1997): task hierarchies with temporal operators (enabling, choice, interleaving, disabling); tool CTTE; W3C considered it for task-model standardisation. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ConcurTaskTrees
- UsiXML: multi-level UI description (task, abstract UI, concrete UI, final UI); top level is a task model with the same goals as CTT. See the CTT/UsiXML/IFML overview in https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.13428 (Interacto)
- IFML 1.0 (OMG, 2015): platform-independent front-end model of view containers, events, interaction flows, based on MOF. https://www.omg.org/ifml/ and https://www.ifml.org/
- [Inference] CTT, UsiXML and IFML describe what the user can do and where flow goes. None of them, in what I read, has a first-class "no scroll", "control always visible" or "completion unmissable" requirement. A contract language would add those as per-state layout and visibility invariants.
- XState model-based testing: `getShortestPaths` and `getSimplePaths` from `xstate/graph` generate event paths over a statechart; v5 `createTestModel` runs them with `path.test()`. v6 splits generation (`xstate/graph`) from execution (`@xstate/test` `testPaths`, fast-check based, coverage assertions such as `assertTestCoverage`). https://stately.ai/docs/xstate/v5/graph , https://stately.ai/docs/xstate/v6/model-based-testing , https://github.com/statelyai/xstate/pull/5752
- [Inference] A statechart gives the set of reachable screens and the shortest path per goal: the exact input for KLM-style "actions per fix" and for generating bot tasks. XState v5 test models reject machines with `invoke`, so effects must be stubbed (per a third-party skill note, [Secondary]).
- Storybook interaction and a11y tests: a story with a `play` function is an interaction test; the a11y addon runs axe after `play` completes (final state); Storybook 9 has `a11y.test: 'error' | 'todo' | 'disabled'`. The old test-runner is deprecated in favour of the Vitest addon. https://storybook.js.org/docs/8/writing-tests/accessibility-testing , https://storybook.js.org/blog/component-test-with-storybook-and-vitest/
- [Inference] `a11y.test: 'todo'` is a built-in debt state: a ratchet vocabulary already exists in Storybook.
- Design tokens: DTCG Format Module 2025.10 is stable (a Final Community Group Report, not a W3C Recommendation). Tokens are JSON with `$value`, `$type`, aliases `{group.token}`, JSON schema at `https://www.designtokens.org/schemas/2025.10/format.json`. https://www.designtokens.org/tr/drafts/format/
- No DTCG-aware stylelint plugin surfaced in my search. [Inference] Token linting means a rule that flags raw hex and magic numbers; the spec supplies the allowed set.

## 4. Layout and visual checks

- CLS: per-shift score = impact fraction x distance fraction. Reported value = largest session window (shifts less than 1 s apart, window at most 5 s). Shifts within 500 ms of user input are excluded (`hadRecentInput`). Good is 0.1 or less. https://web.dev/articles/cls
- [Inference] `hadRecentInput` exclusion matters for a bot gate: a bot-driven click followed by a shift is excluded by default. A UI contract that forbids shift after a bot action must measure with that exclusion off (via `PerformanceObserver` layout-shift entries).
- Galen Framework: `.gspec` files state element positions relative to each other (inside, near, above, width ranges, alignment), per screen size, with `@if/@else` and loops. It runs on Selenium; the last stable release is 2.4.4 (2019), so it looks unmaintained. https://galenframework.com/docs/all/ , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen_Framework
- [Inference] Galen's relative-layout assertions are the closest prior art to a declarative "always visible, no scroll" layout contract. Re-implementing the idea on `getBoundingClientRect()` plus viewport size is small.
- Visual regression flakiness causes: anti-aliasing, subpixel positioning, GPU rasterisation, font hinting, and browser-binary drift. Playwright suffixes baselines with the platform for this reason. Playwright's defaults (`threshold` 0.2 in YIQ space; `maxDiffPixels`, `maxDiffPixelRatio` options) are tunable. Blog guides recommend the official Playwright Docker image for baseline and CI alike. [Secondary] https://bug0.com/knowledge-base/playwright-visual-regression-testing , https://scrolltest.com/visual-regression-tests-fail-ci-playwright-fix/
- Too-loose tolerances hide real regressions (a lost border-radius passes). [Secondary] same sources.
- [Inference] Pixel diffs are poor contract evidence. Geometry assertions (rectangles, overflow, visibility) are deterministic and cheap, so they belong in the contract; screenshots stay as human review aids.

## 5. Metric gaming and ratchets

- Goodhart in UX: the pitfalls named are single-metric tunnel vision, metric sprawl, and vanity metrics. Mitigations: a counterweight metric in the same report, a gaming thought experiment before a KPI is approved, a small set, quarterly pruning. [Secondary] https://quantuxblog.com/how-to-make-heart-metrics-work-in-practice , https://www.ux-guidelines.com/goodharts-law.html . The HEART primary source is Rodden et al. 2010; I did not retrieve it.
- [Inference] Each lower-is-better metric has an obvious cheat: glance cost falls if information is hidden; actions per fix falls if a destructive default is added; layout shift falls if content is withheld. Pair each with a task-success or content-presence check, which is how the hard gates already work.
- Lint ratchet in CI: a committed baseline file of counts; CI fails if a count rises; a separate command lowers the baseline after a real cleanup. [Secondary] https://github.com/chester-hill-solutions/ratchet-strap , https://dev.to/ericgitangu/the-linter-was-grading-the-wrong-checkout-a-lint-baseline-that-can-only-shrink-3plm
- Reported pitfalls: fixing one finding and adding another in the same file leaves the count unchanged; key by file and rule, not line number; compare the baseline file to the base branch, since a baseline nobody checks is a suggestion; local and CI counts can differ (an ignored directory); baselines go stale after many merges. [Secondary] same sources, plus https://github.com/bogdan-alexandrescu/product-oracle/issues/1726
- Lighthouse variability (official): scores change with no code change. The median of 5 runs is about twice as stable as 1 run. Use aggregate values (median, 90th percentile, min/max), do not run concurrent runs on one machine. https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/blob/main/docs/variability.md
- LHCI assertion aggregation: `median`, `optimistic`, `pessimistic`, `median-run`; `numberOfRuns`. Sources disagree on the default, so set it explicitly. https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse-ci/blob/main/docs/configuration.md
- A 4,739-run study [Secondary]: SEO unchanged on 99.9% of consecutive runs, accessibility on 98.7%, performance median within-page SD 8.9 points. https://roughworks.ca/blog/lighthouse-score-variance-4739-runs/ . Byte and request-count budgets are the most stable in CI. [Secondary] https://qaskills.sh/blog/lighthouse-ci-performance-budgets-guide-2026
- [Inference] For a deterministic bot on a fixed viewport, counts (actions, clicks, scrolls, DOM-derived distances) are noise-free and can be ratcheted exactly. Timing-derived metrics need median-of-N plus a tolerance band. Keep the two classes apart in the baseline.

## 6. LLM and vision-model UI evaluation

- Duan et al., CHI 2024: GPT-4 as a heuristic evaluator of 51 UI mockups with three guideline sets. F1 comparable to a single human expert; humans had higher precision and found more global violations and those needing visual understanding; GPT-4 was more thorough. Authors call the two complementary. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.13139
- "Synthetic Heuristic Evaluation" (2025): GPT-4 on ordered screenshots of two apps, Nielsen's 10 heuristics. It covered 73% and 77% of a master issue set, versus 57% and 63% for the pooled output of five experts. It was weak on Consistency and Standards (across-screen issues). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.02306
- "Can GPT-4o evaluate usability like human experts?" (2025): 20 screenshots, 111 issues found, 24.3% false positives; it also hallucinates interaction outcomes from still images. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.16345
- Judge agreement outside usability: GPT-4V vs humans agreement about 71 to 76% on VLFeedback (close to human-human), "more than 50%" vs 33% chance on a generalist evaluation, and weak on subtle visual quality. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.09421 , https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.01361
- WebArena (CMU 2023): 812 tasks on self-hosted sites, checked by answer or final site state. Best GPT-4 agent 14.41% end-to-end, humans 78.24% (5 CS graduate students). Later systems are far higher (61.7% in Feb 2025 per one citing paper), but setups differ. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.13854
- UXAgent (CHI EA 2025): persona-driven LLM agents drive a real browser; outputs include action counts, interviews, video. Intended for piloting a study, not replacing participants. https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12561
- Synthetic Cognitive Walkthrough (2025): LLM agents finished more tasks than humans, on more optimal paths, and found fewer failure points. Over-success hides issues. 6 of 10 humans hit difficulties. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.03568
- Related: SimUser covered 70% of usage scenarios and 80% of usability issues found by 48 humans (cited inside the walkthrough paper, I did not read the original). Agents often fail to recognise task completion (Hawaii paper): https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/5b5d5e9a-62e4-4d0d-bf7b-7106f08a318e/content
- [Inference] The finding that agents often cannot tell a task is complete is direct support for the "unmissable completion" gate: an agent bot that fails to detect completion is a measurable symptom of a missing completion signal.
- [Inference] LLM evaluators fit Triage (propose issues, humans confirm) and not Gate (24% false positives, nondeterminism). A deterministic screen-only bot is the right gate; an LLM persona is a proposal generator.

## Most useful for a UX contract

- Item 3, XState `getShortestPaths`/`getSimplePaths`: state-machine spec gives the reachable screens and the minimal path per goal, feeding both bot task generation and the actions-per-fix count.
- Item 2, `toMatchAriaSnapshot`: a hand-written YAML of roles and names per state is already a declarative, diffable a11y and structure contract, with partial matching and retries.
- Item 4, Galen `.gspec` plus CLS definition: relative and bounding-box layout assertions (no scroll, always-visible) on geometry are deterministic; measure layout shift with `hadRecentInput` handling stated explicitly.
- Item 1, KLM operator model (and Fitts ID from element rectangles): turn a bot trace into operator counts. Use as a ratcheted count, since prediction error is about 20% and it ranks close designs poorly.
- Item 2, ACT rules format: reuse its outcome vocabulary (passed, failed, cantTell, inapplicable) so a contract rule separates "cannot tell" from "pass"; axe covers well under all of WCAG (57% of issues by volume, about a third of SC), so keyboard-only and screen-reader gates must be added explicitly.
- Item 5, ratchet pitfalls: key baselines by rule and scope, check the baseline file against the base branch, make a lowering a separate explicit command, and separate exact (count) metrics from noisy (timing) metrics with median-of-N bands.
- Item 5, Goodhart counterweights: pair every lower-is-better metric with a presence or task-success check so it cannot be met by hiding content.
- Item 6, UXAgent and LLM-judge studies: use LLM personas and VLM review to propose issues in Triage; do not gate on them (false positives, over-success, completion-detection failure). Keep the gate deterministic.
